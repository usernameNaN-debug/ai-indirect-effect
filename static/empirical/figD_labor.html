The tax-preparation workforce shows slow pressure, not a new shock

If consumers stopped buying help with their returns, the first labor-market signal would show up among tax preparers: a seasonal, mostly non-credentialed workforce whose hours rise and fall with the filing calendar. Three public series track it from different angles.

Employment and hiring demand in tax preparation and accounting

Series

Sources: BLS, Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics, May 2019–May 2025; BLS, Current Employment Statistics, tax preparation services (NAICS 541213); Indeed Hiring Lab via FRED (accounting and total job postings indexes); observed AI exposure from Massenkoff and McCrory (2026).

Occupational employment of tax preparers was 76,000 in May 2025, about 6% below 2023, while accountants and auditors held steady at 1.45 million. Employment at tax-preparation firms still peaks every February or March, and the 2026 peak of about 155,000 was the highest since 2020. Indeed postings for accounting jobs ran well above the all-occupation average through 2022. Relative to that average they have fallen steadily since, from 1.28 in January 2023 to 0.99 in September 2026, a decline that began well before AI tax help was common and that the series cannot attribute to any single cause.

Anthropic's measure of observed exposure, which combines what language models can do with what Claude is actually used for, puts tax preparers at 0.13, far below accountants and auditors at 0.35. On current evidence the tax-preparation labor market looks like an industry under slow structural pressure, not one hit by a new shock.
