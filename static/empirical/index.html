The IRS sees no mass shift from paid tax preparation

If chatbots were pulling filers away from paid preparers, the IRS’s weekly counts would show it. They show the opposite: professional e-files rose 0.3% in 2026 and reached 52.0%, the highest share in six seasons. A simulation turns reported cutbacks in paid help into a 9% decline under the full-exit assumption. The IRS result points to changes inside existing filing channels. A middle time-saving simulation values the household gain at 68 million hours, or $1.4 billion, outside GDP.

October 26, 2026

Key takeaways

  • If chatbots were pulling filers away from paid preparers, the IRS’s weekly counts would show it. Professional e-files rose 0.3% in 2026, and their share reached 52.0%, the highest in six seasons.
  • The survey-to-IRS bridge is a simulation. Treating every reported cutback in paid help as a full exit predicts a 9% drop in professional returns; a full-exit share below about one in eight fits the observed count.
  • Surveys put actual or planned AI help for taxes between 11% and 26% of US filers, depending on population and wording. ChatGPT tax queries quadrupled year on year, and Claude tax conversations peaked at eight times a May average on the day before the deadline.
  • In our survey of 1,850 chatbot users, 47% of those using chatbots for money or taxes reported cutting a professional, a paid product, or both. The most common single cut was less unaided effort.
  • A middle simulation values household time saved at 68 million hours a year, or about $1.4 billion. GDP records market output and has no line for unpaid household time.

Why taxes are a good place to look

AI changes more than the work of the firms that build it and the employees who use it. It changes what customers buy, what professionals do, and how households spend their time. This essay follows those indirect effects through tax preparation.

Tax filing offers an annual test. The IRS reports weekly counts of returns e-filed by professionals and taxpayers. In tax year 2024, paid preparers filed 51% of returns, commercial software accounted for 42%, and free government programs and paper made up the rest (Treasury, 2025). The IRS estimates that filing takes households about 2.1 billion hours and costs $49 billion out of pocket each year (IRS burden model, 2024). A return also has a checkable answer. Together these measures track paid services, household time, and accuracy.

We combine a new survey of chatbot users with IRS counts, company filings, AI usage data, and accuracy benchmarks. The analysis follows three outcomes: market spending, household time, and return accuracy.

How many people use AI for their taxes

Survey estimates of actual or planned AI tax use range from 11% to 26%, depending on the population and question. A nationally representative Qlik survey found that 11% of required filers used or planned to use AI for tax help in early 2026. An Adobe survey of full-time workers found that 26% planned to use it for their 2025 returns, up from 11% a year earlier. Gallup found that 18% of adults had sought financial guidance from AI in the past year. Our survey implies that about 16% of adults use a chatbot for money matters. Across sources, AI use is already widespread (fig. 1).

Usage logs show when tax queries concentrate and how quickly they have grown. OpenAI reported that US tax-related queries on ChatGPT were four times higher in the first quarter of 2026 than a year earlier (OpenAI, 2026). In the Anthropic Economic Index, tax-related conversations on April 14 were eight times as common as on an average day in May, and taxation's share of US conversations fell from 0.34% in April to 0.14% in May (Anthropic, 2026). Google's ATLAS study of Gemini conversations found government services and civic obligations, a category led by licenses, fines, fees and taxes, overrepresented almost twentyfold relative to the time Americans spend on them (Google, 2026). Tax filing is one of the most concentrated administrative uses of chatbots.

What AI replaces

To measure what chatbots replace, in August 2026 we asked 1,850 US chatbot users which of ten areas they use their main chatbot for and which inputs they now use less: a professional, a paid product, unpaid help from others, free resources such as tutorials, or their own unaided effort. Finances, budgeting and taxes ranks first on paid inputs (fig. 2). It also has the highest share cutting a paid product or app, putting tax software under the same substitution pressure as professional help.

The reported combinations show what users reduced. About half reported cuts only to unpaid inputs, and the most common single change was less unaided effort (fig. 3). For these users, chatbots replace search, guesswork, and asking a relative. Those time savings create no business revenue.

Use of a chatbot for money rises steeply with overall chatbot use, from 14% among occasional users to 44% among those who use one many times a day. It also rises with subscription spending. The share who then cut back on a professional stays much closer across groups (fig. 4). Adoption is moving faster than substitution among current users.

Did the market move?

The IRS's weekly filing statistics give a direct test of movement away from paid preparation. At the early-May release, professionals e-filed between 50.4% and 51.4% of returns in 2021–2025. In 2026 their share reached 52.0%, the highest in six seasons (fig. 5). Professional e-filing trailed the prior year by 1% to 2% through March, caught up in deadline week, and finished 0.3% ahead.

The survey-to-IRS bridge is a simulation. It starts with all filers and applies the shares who use chatbots, use them for money or taxes, and report hiring a professional less. At a 100% full-exit share, the survey predicts 9% fewer professionally prepared returns (fig. 6). The largest year-on-year change in the three seasons before 2026 was 2.1%. In the simulation, a full-exit share below about one in eight keeps the predicted change within the range of the IRS series.

The simulation points to changes inside existing filing channels: fewer questions to an accountant, a cheaper software tier, or chatbot checks during the same return. Company filings show a matching shift in volume and price (fig. 7). TurboTax handled about 10% fewer units in fiscal 2026 than in fiscal 2022 as revenue rose by a third. H&R Block's assisted returns fell 5% as assisted revenue rose 22%. Intuit's mix shifted toward TurboTax Live, where a human expert prepares or reviews a return; its share of TurboTax revenue rose from about 30% in fiscal 2024 to 53% in fiscal 2026. Intuit's management said on its May 2026 earnings call that it lost price-sensitive do-it-yourself filers earning under $50,000 (Intuit earnings call, 2026). A panel of e-receipts shows TurboTax customers switching to free FreeTaxUSA three times as often as the reverse (YipitData, 2026).

Much of the volume decline predates AI tax help. The 2026 season also brought an 18% rise in refunds after tax-law changes and the closure of the IRS's free Direct File program. Intuit's reported losses came from price-sensitive do-it-yourself filers earning under $50,000, the group with the closest access to free software and chatbot help. Human-assisted service grew at the complex end of the market. A randomized field experiment with 645 Dallas County households found that offering an AI assistant raised the share filing their own property-tax appeal from 41% to 51% (Holz et al., 2026). AI assistance can move tax work into self-service while paid help holds its overall share.

GDP has no line for household time

Market totals record paid services. They leave unpaid household work outside GDP. When a filer handles more of a return with a chatbot, the fee shifts from preparer revenue to household savings. The larger social gain comes from hours freed for both, after accounting for errors. A flat market total can sit alongside a large household time gain.

The two effects can be compared in dollars (fig. 8). The IRS burden model implies 12.6 hours per return and values an hour at about $21. In a middle simulation, 16% of filers use AI and save one-fifth of their time. That returns about 68 million hours a year to households, worth $1.4 billion. A 1% simulated shift from professional preparation to self-filing moves about $170 million in preparer revenue. The full-exit survey simulation moves about $1.5 billion, while the IRS series records a 0.3% increase in professional e-filing.

The IRS estimates 2.1 billion hours of federal tax filing each year, equivalent to the full-time work of about one million people. Brynjolfsson and coauthors' choice experiment values US consumers' access to generative AI at about $172 billion a year, against roughly $14 billion of global consumer spending (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026). That value-to-revenue ratio exceeds ten to one. Tax filing gives the same comparison a concrete measure of household time.

The middle time-saving simulation values household hours at $1.4 billion. A 1% shift between filing channels moves about $170 million in preparer revenue. GDP records the market transaction and leaves the larger household time gain off its books.

Adopters receive the time gains, and adoption is concentrated among heavy chatbot users and subscription customers (fig. 4). Low-cost providers face the revenue pressure. Low-income filers who used a free IRS option lost one when Direct File closed. Access to free filing and accuracy now define the stakes for this group.

Quality and compliance

Accuracy determines whether saved time improves welfare. TaxCalcBench asks models to compute complete federal returns from supplied facts and counts a return correct only when every evaluated line matches. The best model in July 2025 got 32% of test returns exactly right. Models released since then reach 63% on the same set. On a harder 2025 set with realistic document inputs, the best reach 70% to 78% when allowed to search the web (fig. 9). Line-level accuracy is higher, so one or two errors can make an otherwise correct return wrong.

Existing filing options set the benchmark for chatbot accuracy. Random IRS audits show that paid preparers increase underreported income by roughly $3,900 per return relative to self-preparation (DeBacker et al., 2024). The GAO found error rates of 60% on paid-prepared returns and 50% on self-prepared returns in earlier audit data (GAO, 2026). IRS capacity to answer questions has also fallen: staffing in fiscal 2026 is about a quarter lower than before (National Taxpayer Advocate, 2026a), and live assistors answered 20% fewer calls in the 2026 season (National Taxpayer Advocate, 2026b). Chatbots now serve some of the demand the agency leaves unanswered.

Respondents who cut back on professionals for money matters report the largest savings. They report incorrect chatbot information as often as other finance users and more often than respondents in the no-finance-use group (fig. 10). Heavy users report more of both outcomes. Savings and error exposure therefore appear together among the people reducing paid help.

Preparers and the learning pipeline

Labor indicators show gradual pressure across tax preparation (fig. 11). Occupational employment of tax preparers was 76,000 in May 2025, about 6% below 2023, while accountants and auditors held steady at 1.45 million. Seasonal employment at tax-preparation firms peaked at about 155,000 in early 2026, the highest since 2020. Indeed postings for accounting jobs fell from well above the all-occupation average to roughly even with it, a trend that began in early 2023. Anthropic's observed-exposure measure combines model capability with actual Claude use; it puts tax preparers at 0.13 and accountants at 0.35 (Massenkoff and McCrory, 2026).

The key labor signal is who gets which work. Simple returns train new preparers and junior accountants. As software and chatbots handle more of those cases, young workers lose practice even while demand for experienced, complex work holds up. Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen find that employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, including accounting, was about 19% below trend by mid-2026; older workers showed no comparable gap (Brynjolfsson et al., 2026). The training pipeline is the long-run labor effect to watch.

What to measure next

Three data additions would give the next estimate a direct footing. A 2027 survey should record how each input changed, along with minutes and dollars, to separate full exits from smaller purchases. A linked sample of receipts or preparer records could verify spending, while reference calculations measure errors in AI-assisted returns. The IRS could add a checkbox for AI assistance and report it annually, as proposed by the Tax Policy Center (Tax Policy Center, 2026).

The lesson extends beyond taxes. When households use technology to replace purchases or shorten unpaid tasks, early gains appear in household time and in the mix of services people buy. GDP records paid activity, so industry totals can stay flat while household welfare rises.

Data and methods

The survey ran on Prolific with US adults from August 22–25, 2026. Of 2,167 approved responses, 1,850 current chatbot users passed reCAPTCHA, duration, and two attention screens. For each of ten personal-use domains a respondent selected, we asked: “Since you started using your most-used chatbot for [domain], which do you do less?” Respondents could select multiple options. The analytic sample consists of current chatbot users, with heavier chatbot use than the US adult population; figure 6 lets readers vary the population shares. Intervals are 95% Wilson intervals.

IRS filing-season statistics were collected from each weekly release from 2021 to 2026. Each release reports cumulative counts for the current season and the comparable week of the prior season; we use each season's own release nearest May 4 for level comparisons and the within-release ratio for year-on-year growth. Company figures come from SEC filings; where a firm restated a series, we chain the new series onto the old at the overlap year. All code and aggregated data used in the figures are available on request.
